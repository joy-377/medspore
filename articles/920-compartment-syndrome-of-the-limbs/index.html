Compartment Syndrome of the Limbs

The 5 Ps, intracompartmental pressure monitoring, and emergency fasciotomy...

Final Year (Part 2) · Orthopedics

Pathophysiology:
Increased tissue pressure within a closed osteofascial compartment leads to microvascular collapse, muscle and nerve ischemia. Most commonly occurs in the anterior compartment of the leg following tibial shaft fractures, crush injuries, or tight circumferential casts.

The Classical '5 Ps' (Clinical Presentation):
1. Pain out of proportion to the injury (earliest and most reliable sign). Pain significantly aggravated by passive stretch of the muscles in that compartment.
2. Pallor (though pulses are usually preserved because capillary pressure is lower than arterial pressure).
3. Pulselessness (a very late, ominous sign indicating complete vascular shutdown).
4. Paresthesia (early nerve ischemia sign, e.g., loss of first web space sensation in anterior tibial compartment syndrome).
5. Paralysis (latest sign indicating irreversible muscle/nerve death).

Diagnostic & Therapeutic Standard:
• Intracompartmental pressure measurement using a slit catheter or Stryker pressure monitor. Absolute pressure > 30–40 mmHg (or delta pressure < 30 mmHg relative to diastolic BP) mandates Emergency Surgical Fasciotomy (decompression of all compartments via dual-incision technique).
