Arterial Blood Gas Interpretation: A Stepwise Acid–Base Method

Start with pH, identify the primary disturbance, check expected compensation and avoid missing a mixed acid–base disorder.

Quick Revision

  • Interpret pH and PaCO₂/HCO₃⁻ as a set.
  • Use predicted compensation to detect mixed disorders.
  • Calculate and interpret anion gap in metabolic acidosis.
  • Reference intervals depend on methods and clinical context.
01

Read the pH and paired values

  • Read arterial pH, PaCO₂ and bicarbonate together; avoid interpreting isolated values.
  • As a commonly taught adult reference interval, arterial pH is approximately 7.35–7.45, but laboratory methods and patient context matter.
  • A pH near normal does not exclude a mixed acid–base disorder.
02

Identify the primary process

  • Acidemia with low bicarbonate points toward a metabolic acidosis; acidemia with high PaCO₂ suggests respiratory acidosis.
  • Alkalemia with high bicarbonate suggests metabolic alkalosis; alkalemia with low PaCO₂ suggests respiratory alkalosis.
  • Clinical history and measured serum chemistry results are essential.
03

Compensation: ask whether it fits

  • Compensatory changes are predictable only within approximate physiologic ranges.
  • In metabolic acidosis, Winter’s formula estimates expected PaCO₂: 1.5 × serum HCO₃⁻ + 8 (± 2 mmHg).
  • Measured PaCO₂ outside the expected range suggests an additional respiratory disorder.
04

Examine the anion gap

  • For metabolic acidosis, calculate anion gap using sodium − (chloride + bicarbonate) in consistent units.
  • Interpret anion gap with albumin concentration, electrolyte measurement technique and clinical context.
05

Memory aid

  • ABG order: pH first → primary respiratory/metabolic process → expected compensation → anion gap when indicated.
  • Check oxygenation and the whole clinical picture separately.
06

Exam trap: one value does not tell the whole story

  • Compensation alone rarely explains pH overcorrection across normal.
  • Multiple acid–base disturbances can coexist, especially in critically ill patients.
