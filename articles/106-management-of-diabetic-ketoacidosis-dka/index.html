Diabetic Ketoacidosis: Fluids, Potassium and Insulin — Clinical Logic

DKA requires recognition of ketosis and metabolic acidosis, careful fluid replacement and potassium-aware insulin therapy.

Quick Revision

  • DKA is defined by a compatible diabetes or hyperglycemia context, ketonemia and metabolic acidosis; euglycemic presentations can occur.
  • Restore circulation, assess serum potassium and start appropriate insulin treatment with frequent monitoring.
  • Glucose may improve before ketoacidosis resolves; assess resolution using ketones and the acid–base picture.
01

Recognize the biochemical syndrome

  • Assess clinical hydration, mental status, respiratory effort and any precipitating infection or missed insulin.
  • Do not rely on blood glucose alone: some DKA cases present without very high glucose, particularly with SGLT2-inhibitor exposure.
02

Follow the physiological priorities

  • Fluid resuscitation, potassium assessment and insulin are linked decisions; marked potassium depletion must be addressed before insulin is started.
  • Use a locally approved adult or pediatric protocol for rates, concentrations and potassium replacement.
03

Avoid early treatment errors

  • Insulin can move potassium into cells and precipitate dangerous hypokalemia if potassium deficits are overlooked.
  • A falling glucose reading does not establish correction of ketoacidosis; ongoing dextrose may be required as insulin continues.
