Syncope: Cardiac vs Neurological vs Vasovagal

How to differentiate the cause of sudden loss of consciousness at the bedside...

Clinical Tips · General Tips

Learning note: Educational revision content; verify medication doses, protocols and reference ranges against current institutional guidelines before any patient-care use.

Syncope is a transient loss of consciousness due to global cerebral hypoperfusion. A careful history can often pinpoint the exact cause:

  • Vasovagal (Neurocardiogenic) Syncope: Often has a prodrome (nausea, sweating, blurred vision, ringing in ears) and is triggered by prolonged standing, emotional stress, or pain. Recovery is rapid once supine.
  • Cardiac (Arrhythmogenic) Syncope: Occurs suddenly without any warning or prodrome. Can happen while sitting or supine. Often associated with palpitations or chest pain. High risk of sudden cardiac death.
  • Orthostatic Hypotension: Occurs exactly upon standing up from a sitting or lying position. Triggered by volume depletion, alpha-blockers, or autonomic neuropathy (Diabetes/Parkinson's).
  • Seizures (Neurological): Preceded by an aura, followed by tonic-clonic movements, tongue biting, urinary incontinence, and a prolonged post-ictal state (confusion lasting minutes to hours).
