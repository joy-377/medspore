Emergency & Trauma Scores: GCS, Wells & Parkland

Recognize the Glasgow Coma Scale, the clinical probability role of the Wells score, and why modern burn fluid resuscitation is titrated.

Quick Revision

  • GCS assesses eye opening, verbal response and motor response; individual components and the clinical context matter.
  • Wells criteria estimate pretest probability of pulmonary embolism and help direct appropriate testing.
  • Burn fluid formulas are starting estimates; resuscitation should respond to patient physiology and specialist guidance.
01

Neurologic assessment with GCS

  • Record eye, verbal and motor components separately and repeat assessments over time.
  • A low GCS prompts urgent airway assessment but does not replace evaluation of airway reflexes, oxygenation and other causes of reduced consciousness.
02

Pulmonary embolism risk before testing

  • Use Wells or another validated rule in the intended population, incorporating clinical context, D-dimer and imaging when appropriate.
  • Probability estimates are less reliable when used outside their validated setting.
03

Burns: titrate rather than blindly follow a formula

  • For adults with large burns, the American Burn Association recommends a lower initial fluid estimate than the historical Parkland 4 mL/kg/%TBSA approach, to reduce fluid-related harm.
  • Repeated urine-output and perfusion assessments guide adjustments in a specialized care pathway.
04

Memory aid

  • GCS components: E (eye), V (verbal), M (motor).
  • Record each component rather than only a summed total.
05

Exam trap

  • Do not treat a historical burn-fluid formula as an unchangeable prescription.
  • Neither a low GCS nor a positive probability score replaces professional clinical assessment.
